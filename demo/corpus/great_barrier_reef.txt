The Great Barrier Reef is the world's largest coral reef system, lying in the Coral Sea off the coast of Queensland in north-eastern Australia. It stretches for more than 2,300 kilometres and is made up of thousands of individual reefs and hundreds of islands built from the skeletons of billions of tiny animals called coral polyps. The reef is so vast that it can be seen from space, and it is often described as the largest structure ever built by living organisms. It supports an extraordinary diversity of marine life, including many species of fish, molluscs, sea turtles, dolphins, and sharks, as well as the corals themselves. Corals obtain much of their energy from microscopic algae that live inside their tissues, giving them their bright colours. When water temperatures rise too high, the corals expel these algae and turn white in a process called coral bleaching, which can kill them if stressful conditions persist. Warming oceans, pollution, and outbreaks of the crown-of-thorns starfish have all damaged parts of the reef in recent decades. The Great Barrier Reef was designated a World Heritage Site in 1981 in recognition of its outstanding natural value. It is also a major tourist destination, drawing visitors who come to dive and snorkel among the corals, and it contributes substantially to the Australian economy. The reef built up over many thousands of years as generation after generation of coral grew upon the limestone skeletons of those that came before. Protecting it involves limiting harmful fishing, improving the quality of water flowing from rivers, and studying corals that may better tolerate warmer seas.
