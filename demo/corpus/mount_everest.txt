Mount Everest is the highest mountain on Earth when measured from sea level, reaching an elevation of about 8,849 metres. It lies in the Himalaya range along the border between Nepal and the Tibet Autonomous Region of China. The mountain is known as Sagarmatha in Nepali and as Chomolungma in Tibetan, names that reflect its cultural importance to the peoples living nearby. Everest was first climbed to the summit on the twenty-ninth of May 1953 by Edmund Hillary of New Zealand and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa mountaineer, as part of a British expedition. Climbers usually attempt the peak by one of two main routes, the South Col route from Nepal or the North Col route from Tibet. Above roughly 8,000 metres lies the so-called death zone, where the air holds so little oxygen that the human body slowly deteriorates and cannot acclimatise. For this reason most climbers carry bottled oxygen, though a few have reached the top without it. Avalanches, sudden storms, deep crevasses, and extreme cold make the ascent dangerous, and many climbers have died on its slopes. Despite the risks, hundreds of people now reach the summit each year during short weather windows, guided by experienced expedition teams. The mountain continues to rise slowly as the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates push against each other. The mountain was named in the nineteenth century after George Everest, a former surveyor general of India, although he himself objected to the choice. Local guides known as Sherpas play an essential part in most expeditions, carrying heavy loads and fixing ropes along the route.
