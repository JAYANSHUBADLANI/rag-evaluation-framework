A lithium-ion battery is a type of rechargeable battery in which lithium ions carry charge by moving back and forth between two electrodes. During discharge, lithium ions travel from the negative electrode, or anode, through a liquid electrolyte to the positive electrode, or cathode, while electrons flow through the external circuit and do useful work. When the battery is charged, an applied voltage drives the ions and electrons back in the opposite direction, restoring the original arrangement. Compared with older rechargeable chemistries, lithium-ion cells store a large amount of energy for their weight, a property known as high energy density, and they lose their charge only slowly when not in use. These qualities have made them the standard power source for portable electronics such as smartphones, tablets, and laptop computers, and they are central to the batteries of electric vehicles. Large arrays of lithium-ion cells are also used to store electricity from solar and wind installations for release when demand is high. The technology was developed through the work of several scientists, and John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham, and Akira Yoshino were awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2019 for their contributions. Careful management is required because the cells can overheat if damaged or overcharged, so batteries include electronic circuits that monitor voltage and temperature. Research continues into new materials that promise greater capacity, faster charging, and improved safety. The performance of a cell gradually declines over hundreds or thousands of charging cycles as its internal materials slowly degrade. Recycling used lithium-ion batteries recovers valuable metals and reduces the need to mine fresh supplies from the ground.
