Photosynthesis is the biological process by which green plants, algae, and certain bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in sugars. It takes place mainly in the chloroplasts of plant cells, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll that absorbs light most strongly in the blue and red parts of the spectrum. The overall reaction combines carbon dioxide drawn from the air with water taken up by the roots, using energy from sunlight to produce glucose and oxygen. This is often summarised by the equation six carbon dioxide plus six water plus light energy yields one glucose molecule plus six oxygen molecules. Photosynthesis proceeds in two connected stages. In the light-dependent reactions, which occur in the thylakoid membranes, absorbed light splits water molecules, releases oxygen as a byproduct, and stores energy in the carrier molecules ATP and NADPH. In the light-independent reactions, known as the Calvin cycle and taking place in the stroma, that stored energy is used to fix carbon dioxide into carbohydrate. Because it produces both food and oxygen, photosynthesis underpins almost every food chain on Earth and helps maintain the oxygen-rich atmosphere on which animals depend. Plants that carry out photosynthesis are called producers because they manufacture their own food rather than consuming other organisms. The rate of photosynthesis is influenced by light intensity, the concentration of carbon dioxide, and temperature, each of which can become a limiting factor. Some plants in hot, dry climates use variants of the process, such as C4 and CAM photosynthesis, that reduce water loss while still capturing carbon. On a global scale, photosynthesis carried out by ocean plankton and by land plants removes vast amounts of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere every year.
