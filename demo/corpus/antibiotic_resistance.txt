Antibiotic resistance is the ability of bacteria to survive exposure to drugs that were once able to kill them or stop their growth. It arises through the ordinary process of natural selection: within a large population of bacteria, chance mutations or genes acquired from other microbes may allow a few individuals to withstand a drug. When an antibiotic is applied, the susceptible bacteria die while the resistant ones survive and multiply, so that resistance spreads through later generations. Bacteria can also share resistance genes directly with one another on small loops of DNA called plasmids, which lets resistance jump between different species. The overuse and misuse of antibiotics, such as taking them for viral illnesses they cannot cure or failing to finish a prescribed course, speeds up this selection. Their heavy use in agriculture and livestock adds further pressure that favours resistant strains. Well-known resistant organisms include methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, often abbreviated MRSA, which is difficult to treat with common drugs. As more bacteria become resistant, once-routine infections grow harder and more expensive to cure, and some may become untreatable. Health authorities regard antibiotic resistance as one of the most serious threats to modern medicine. Efforts to slow it include using antibiotics only when necessary, improving hygiene and infection control, and investing in the development of new drugs and vaccines. Some bacteria resist drugs by pumping them back out, by altering the target that the drug attacks, or by producing enzymes that break the drug apart. International health bodies now track resistant infections and urge careful stewardship of the antibiotics that still work.
