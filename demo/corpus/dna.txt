Deoxyribonucleic acid, usually shortened to DNA, is the molecule that carries the genetic instructions used in the growth, development, and functioning of living organisms. Its now-famous double helix structure, resembling a twisted ladder, was described in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick, who built on the X-ray diffraction images produced by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins. A DNA molecule is a long chain built from repeating units called nucleotides, each containing a sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four nitrogen-containing bases. The four bases are adenine, thymine, guanine, and cytosine, usually abbreviated A, T, G, and C. The two strands of the helix are held together by pairs of bases, with adenine always pairing with thymine and guanine always pairing with cytosine, a rule known as complementary base pairing. Because of this pairing, each strand carries all the information needed to rebuild the other, which allows DNA to be copied faithfully when cells divide. The particular order of the bases along a strand spells out the genetic code, and stretches of this sequence that specify proteins are called genes. The complete set of DNA in an organism is known as its genome. Errors in copying, or damage from radiation and chemicals, can change the sequence and produce mutations, some of which are harmless while others cause disease. Understanding DNA has transformed biology, medicine, and forensic science. In the cells of plants and animals, DNA is packaged together with proteins into structures called chromosomes that are copied and shared out when a cell divides. The ability to read, or sequence, the order of the bases has made it possible to compare whole genomes and trace relationships between species.
