Photovoltaics is the technology that converts sunlight directly into electricity using devices called solar cells. Each cell is made from a semiconductor material, most commonly silicon, that has been specially treated so that it develops an internal electric field. The conversion relies on the photovoltaic effect: when particles of light, or photons, strike the semiconductor, they transfer their energy to electrons and free them to move, and the built-in field pushes these electrons in a consistent direction to create an electric current. A single cell produces only a small voltage, so many cells are wired together and sealed behind protective glass to form a solar panel, and panels in turn are combined into larger arrays. Because the process uses no fuel and has no moving parts, photovoltaic systems generate power silently and without producing emissions or waste while they operate. Typical commercial panels convert somewhere between about fifteen and twenty-two percent of the sunlight that falls on them into electricity, and their efficiency has improved steadily over time. The electricity produced is direct current, so an inverter is usually added to convert it into the alternating current used by homes and the power grid. Solar power can be installed at scales ranging from a single rooftop to vast utility power stations covering many hectares. Falling manufacturing costs have made photovoltaics one of the fastest-growing sources of renewable energy in the world. The amount of electricity a panel produces depends on the strength of the sunlight, the angle at which the light strikes the surface, and the temperature of the cells. Batteries are often paired with photovoltaic systems so that power gathered during the day can be used after sunset.
