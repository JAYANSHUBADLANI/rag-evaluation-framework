The Internet Protocol, abbreviated IP, is the principal set of rules that governs how data is sent across the boundaries between networks so that it can travel throughout the internet. Its central job is to divide information into small units called packets and to give every device an address, known as an IP address, that identifies where packets should be delivered. Each packet is routed independently, passing from one router to the next as each device reads the destination address and forwards the packet onward toward its goal. The Internet Protocol is usually paired with the Transmission Control Protocol, and together they form the widely used suite referred to as TCP/IP. In this partnership IP handles addressing and delivery, while TCP arranges the packets in the correct order, checks for errors, and requests that any missing pieces be sent again. The older version of the protocol still in broad use, IPv4, writes addresses as thirty-two-bit numbers, which allows for roughly four billion distinct addresses. Because the growth of connected devices exhausted that supply, a newer version called IPv6 was introduced, using one-hundred-twenty-eight-bit addresses to provide an almost unlimited pool. IP itself makes no guarantee that a packet will arrive, treating delivery as a best effort, which is why the accompanying protocols supply reliability when it is needed. This simple and flexible design has allowed the internet to grow to connect billions of devices worldwide. To make long numeric addresses easier for people to use, a separate naming system translates memorable names into the numbers that machines actually route by. Because packets may take different paths and arrive out of order, the cooperating protocols put them back into the correct sequence at the destination.
