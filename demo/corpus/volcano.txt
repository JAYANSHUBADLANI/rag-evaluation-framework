A volcano is an opening in the crust of a planet through which molten rock, hot gases, and fragments of solid material can escape from below the surface. Beneath the ground, rock that has melted is called magma, and once it erupts and flows across the surface it is known as lava. Most volcanoes form near the boundaries of the tectonic plates that make up the outer shell of the Earth, either where plates pull apart or where one plate sinks beneath another. A large number of volcanoes ring the Pacific Ocean along a zone of intense geological activity known as the Ring of Fire. Volcanoes take different shapes depending on how they erupt; broad, gently sloping shield volcanoes are built by runny lava, while steep-sided stratovolcanoes are constructed from alternating layers of lava and ash from more explosive eruptions. Eruptions can be highly destructive, burying land under lava, blanketing regions with ash, and sending fast-moving flows of hot gas and debris down their slopes. At the same time, volcanic activity builds new land, and entire island chains such as Hawaii have been created by eruptions over millions of years. Volcanic ash also weathers into fertile soils that support agriculture in many regions. Scientists monitor volcanoes for warning signs such as earthquakes and swelling of the ground in order to forecast eruptions and protect nearby communities. The scientific study of volcanoes is called volcanology, and the researchers who practise it are known as volcanologists. Very large eruptions can even affect the climate, as ash and gas thrown high into the atmosphere reflect sunlight and cool the surface for a time.
