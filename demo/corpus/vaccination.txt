Vaccination is the deliberate administration of a vaccine in order to stimulate the immune system to build protection against a particular disease. A vaccine typically contains a weakened or inactivated form of a pathogen, or just a characteristic piece of it, which cannot cause serious illness but which the body learns to recognise. After vaccination the immune system produces antibodies and memory cells, so that if the real pathogen is encountered later it can be attacked quickly before it causes disease. The practice has its roots in the work of the English physician Edward Jenner, who in 1796 showed that inoculating a person with cowpox could protect them against the far deadlier smallpox. The word vaccine itself derives from the Latin word for cow, in memory of that first experiment. Over the following two centuries vaccines were developed against many diseases, including polio, measles, tetanus, and influenza. A worldwide vaccination campaign led to the eradication of smallpox, which was declared eliminated in 1980, the first disease ever wiped out by human effort. When a large enough fraction of a population is immune, the spread of a disease slows or stops, protecting even those who cannot be vaccinated; this effect is called herd immunity. Vaccination is regarded as one of the most cost-effective measures in public health. Ongoing programmes aim to extend the benefits of vaccines to children everywhere. Modern vaccines take many forms, from inactivated viruses to purified proteins and, more recently, genetic instructions that prompt cells to make a harmless piece of a pathogen. Keeping many vaccines effective requires an unbroken chain of refrigeration from the factory all the way to the clinic.
