The honeybee is a flying insect of the genus Apis, best known for building perennial nests and producing honey and beeswax. The most widespread species is the western honeybee, Apis mellifera, which is kept by beekeepers around the world. A honeybee colony is a highly organised society made up of a single reproductive queen, tens of thousands of sterile female workers, and a smaller number of male drones that appear seasonally. Worker bees perform nearly all the tasks of the hive, including cleaning cells, feeding larvae, building wax comb, guarding the entrance, and foraging for nectar and pollen. When a forager discovers a rich source of food, it returns to the hive and performs a waggle dance, a figure-eight movement whose angle and duration encode the direction and distance of the flowers. Honey is made from nectar that workers gather, partially digest, and then dehydrate inside wax cells until it thickens and can be stored for long periods. Honeybees are among the most important pollinators of flowering plants, and many fruit and vegetable crops depend on their visits to set seed. In recent decades beekeepers have reported episodes of colony collapse disorder, in which most of the worker bees abandon a hive, raising concern about pollinator health. Pollination services provided by honeybees contribute significantly to agriculture and to the reproduction of wild plants. A queen may lay well over a thousand eggs in a single day at the height of summer, and she can live for several years, far longer than the few weeks typical of a summer worker. Beekeepers manage hives in stacked wooden boxes, harvesting surplus honey while leaving enough for the colony to survive the winter.
