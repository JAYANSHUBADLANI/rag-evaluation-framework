Penicillin was the first antibiotic to be used widely in medicine, and it is produced by moulds of the genus Penicillium. Its discovery is credited to the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming, who in 1928 noticed that a mould contaminating one of his laboratory dishes had killed the surrounding colonies of bacteria. Fleming identified a substance released by the mould, which he named penicillin, but he was unable to purify it in useful quantities. More than a decade later, a team at Oxford led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain developed methods to concentrate and produce the drug, and they demonstrated its power to cure bacterial infections in patients. Their work turned penicillin into a practical medicine just as the Second World War created an urgent need to treat wounded soldiers. Penicillin works by interfering with the construction of bacterial cell walls, so that growing bacteria burst and die, while human cells, which lack such walls, are unharmed. The drug proved effective against many serious infections, including pneumonia and blood poisoning, that had often been fatal before. For their achievement, Fleming, Florey, and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945. The success of penicillin launched the modern age of antibiotics and encouraged the search for many other compounds able to fight disease-causing microbes. It remains an important medicine, though some bacteria have since developed ways to resist it. Because the human body clears the drug quickly, early doses were sometimes recovered from patients and reused when supplies were desperately scarce. Penicillin belongs to a family of related antibiotics that share a chemical feature known as the beta-lactam ring.
