A Roman aqueduct was an engineered structure built to carry fresh water from distant springs and rivers into the towns and cities of the ancient Roman world. The system relied on gravity: engineers surveyed a route that descended along a very gentle and continuous slope, so that water flowed steadily downhill over long distances. Although the arched bridges that march across valleys are the most famous image of aqueducts, most of the length of a typical aqueduct actually ran underground in covered channels or through tunnels cut into hillsides. Where a valley had to be crossed, the Romans raised the channel on tiers of stone or brick arches to keep the water at the required height. The first such aqueduct, the Aqua Appia, was constructed in Rome in 312 BC, and over the following centuries many more were added to supply the growing capital. Inside the cities the water fed public fountains, drinking basins, and the great public baths, and it flushed the drains and latrines that kept streets cleaner. Wealthier households could pay to have water piped directly to their homes, while the poor collected it from street fountains. One of the best-preserved aqueduct bridges is the Pont du Gard in southern France, whose three rows of arches still span a river valley. The skill embodied in these structures allowed Roman cities to grow larger and healthier than earlier settlements had been able to. Settling tanks placed along the route let dirt and debris sink out of the water before it reached the city. Roman engineers lined the covered channels with waterproof mortar and regularly cleared them of the mineral deposits left behind by the flowing water.
