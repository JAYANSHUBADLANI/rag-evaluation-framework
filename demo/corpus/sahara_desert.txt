The Sahara is the largest hot desert in the world, spreading across much of North Africa. It covers an area of roughly 9.2 million square kilometres, which is comparable in size to the United States or China. The desert stretches from the Atlantic Ocean in the west to the Red Sea in the east, and it touches or crosses the territory of about a dozen countries. Although many people picture the Sahara as an endless sea of sand, dunes actually make up only part of it; large expanses consist of rocky plateaus, gravel plains, dry valleys, and salt flats. Daytime temperatures in summer regularly climb above 40 degrees Celsius, while clear desert skies allow the heat to escape at night, so temperatures can fall sharply after dark. Rainfall is extremely low and unpredictable, and some areas may go years without a significant shower. Despite these harsh conditions, the Sahara supports specially adapted plants and animals, as well as human communities clustered around oases where underground water reaches the surface. To the south the desert gives way to a semi-arid belt of grassland known as the Sahel, which forms a transition to the wetter lands beyond. Evidence from rock art shows that thousands of years ago parts of the Sahara were far greener and supported lakes and grazing animals. Great caravans once crossed the Sahara carrying salt, gold, and other goods between North and West Africa. Today the desert is also studied because dust blown from it travels across the Atlantic and helps fertilise distant forests and oceans.
