The printing press is a mechanical device that applies pressure to transfer ink from a surface of movable type onto paper or another medium. Around the year 1440, in the German city of Mainz, the goldsmith Johannes Gutenberg introduced a printing press that used individually cast metal letters which could be arranged, inked, and reused. His system combined several innovations, including a durable metal type alloy, an oil-based ink that clung to metal, and a screw press adapted from those used for pressing grapes and olives. One of the earliest major works produced with his method was the Gutenberg Bible, printed in the 1450s and prized for its quality. Before this invention, books in Europe were copied slowly by hand or printed laboriously from carved wooden blocks, which made them scarce and costly. The movable-type press allowed texts to be reproduced quickly and in large numbers, dramatically lowering the price of books. As printed material spread, literacy grew and new ideas travelled farther and faster than ever before. Historians link the press to the rapid diffusion of Renaissance learning and to the Protestant Reformation, whose pamphlets and translated scriptures reached wide audiences. The technology spread across Europe within a few decades, and by 1500 hundreds of towns had printing workshops. The printing press is often counted among the most influential inventions in human history. Workers called compositors set the individual letters into lines and pages by hand, a painstaking craft that survived for centuries. Later inventions, such as the steam-powered press and the rotary press, multiplied printing speeds and made cheap newspapers possible.
