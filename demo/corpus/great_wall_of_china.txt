The Great Wall of China is a series of fortifications built across the northern frontier of China to guard against raids and invasions by nomadic peoples from the steppes. Rather than a single continuous barrier, it is a network of walls, watchtowers, and forts constructed and rebuilt by many dynasties over more than two thousand years. Early rulers joined and extended existing walls as far back as the third century BC, but most of the wall that survives today was built during the Ming dynasty between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries. The builders used whatever materials were close at hand, so sections are made variously of rammed earth, stone, and brick, with the sturdier Ming portions faced in cut stone and fired brick. Watchtowers set along the wall allowed guards to keep lookout and to send warnings by smoke or fire when enemies approached. Estimates of the total length of all the branches and sections run to many thousands of kilometres, winding across mountains, deserts, and grasslands. Beyond its military role, the wall helped regulate trade and the movement of people along the frontier. Contrary to a popular myth, the wall cannot in fact be seen with the unaided eye from the Moon. Today the Great Wall is one of the most recognised monuments in the world and was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987. Millions of visitors walk its restored sections each year. Soldiers stationed in garrisons along the wall could be supplied and moved quickly using the roadway that ran along the top of many sections. Gates through the wall served as controlled crossing points where officials could tax passing merchants and inspect travellers.
